## How the classes were applied

- A hedge counts wherever it stands; the section is named. "Only unsure" means the thing is not in the description or inventory at all.
- 1 WEIGHT. Five E readings give the weight inside a "seems stuck ... because he is heavier" clause (reader 1 three times, reader 2 pic_061, reader 12 pic_004). The "seems" is counted against STUCK, not against WEIGHT. Counted against both, E's weight would be 11 plain and 8 hedged (still 19), and reader 1 would have no plain weight. Reader 10 pic_050 states the weight plainly in the description and then sets it against teasing in the unsure section: counted hedged. Reader 3 pic_082 has it under "as if" in the description and unhedged in the unsure section ("The tilt fits the weights"): counted plain.
- 2 STUCK. "holding her up" (E reader 9, twice) and "keeps her up" (B reader 15, once) are counted, as the brief lists held up and kept up; both are the bodily sense, with no word for a plight. "lifted high" and "raised high" (B reader 15) and "keeping his side down" (said of the man's end, E reader 3) are not counted.
- 3 HER STATE. Plain means a word standing alone (alarmed, uneasy, unhappy). The pair "surprised or worried" and any "could be fear or just surprise" are counted as hedged. "mouth open" and "talking" are not counted as calling; they are listed apart.
- 4 ON PURPOSE. Counted when a purpose is offered (tease, deliberately) or "holding the seesaw down" is set against "simply resting". A bare "keeping his side down" or "holding the seesaw down" in the description, with no purpose word, is counted apart as near-4.
- 5 ORDINARY PLAY. Counted when a game or normal use is named (playing, the seesaw going up and down, normal for a seesaw, an ordinary afternoon). A tilt called "as expected", "fits the weights", "makes sense" with no game named is counted apart as near-5. A bare report with none of classes 1 to 5 is counted apart too.
- By reader: a reader is counted when any of its three readings has the thing. In the "no" rows a reader is counted when none of its readings has it.

## Closing paragraph

Stuck or kept up is said in 20 of E's 24 readings (7 of 8 readers; the word "stuck" in 18, "holding her up" in 2) and in 1 of B's 24 (1 of 8 readers: "his weight keeps her up in the air", in a reading that also calls it "an ordinary afternoon at the playground"); no B reading has the word "stuck". E's stuck is hedged more often than not: 6 readings plain (3 readers), 8 hedged in the description ("seems", "looks as if", "as if", "possibly") and 6 only in the unsure section. His weight is given about as often in both arms (E 19 of 24, B 14 of 24, all 8 readers in each), so the weight alone does not part the arms; what differs is what it explains: in E it stands with stuck in 18 readings (7 readers; both plain in 4, 3 readers), in B it explains a tilt that "fits", "makes sense" or is "as expected" (10 of the 14) and stands with class 2 once. A worried or frightened girl is in 18 E readings (6 readers), nearly always as the pair "surprised or worried" with a "fear or just surprise / just talking" either-or in the unsure section, and in 1 B reading ("content or slightly concerned", unsure section); in neither arm is she said to call, shout or ask to be let down. An ordinary game is named in 3 E readings, all by one reader and each time as one of two in the unsure section, and in 4 B readings by 4 readers, each time as the reading; with the "as expected / fits the weights" remarks added it is E 6 (3 readers) against B 11 (7 readers), and bare reports with none of classes 1 to 5 are E 3 against B 10. E's stretched arm is remarked in 9 readings by 3 readers, as pointing or as "holding the handle or pointing", never as pleading, and the 15 E readings that do not remark it all have both stuck and the worried look (against 5 and 3 of the 9 that do); in B 5 readings by 2 readers put the girl in front of or off her seat and none remarks her lean back. What does not differ: the man's seat draws the same kind of remark in both arms (legs overlapping the beam, E 4 readings by 2 readers, B 2 by 1; nobody puts him off his seat), and a purpose is offered only with a hedge in the unsure section (E 2, B 1). The numbers cannot show how much is the drawing and how much the readers, since each arm has its own eight readers and every habit counts three times (E's three "just playing" are one reader, its three "pointing" another), though the stuck gap holds by reader (7 of 8 against 1 of 8) as well as by reading.
